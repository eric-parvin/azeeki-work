# Primes vs. Zooms — Talk Summary
### A veteran sports/portrait photographer's take on lens choice, technique, and working fast

The photographer (decades of experience, including Sports Illustrated work and long-running university/corporate contracts) walked through his lens history and current kit while showing images, then took audience questions on autofocus technique. The overall theme: modern fast zooms have made most of his prime lenses obsolete for his kind of work.

---

## 1. Favorite Lenses

**Sony 50–150mm f/2 GM — his single favorite lens, by far.**
He calls it "a prime that zooms." It's constant f/2 across the whole range, which he says no other manufacturer currently offers in this focal range (Canon and Nikon shooters don't have an equivalent yet). Uses it for basically everything:
- Court/close-range sports — basketball, volleyball, wrestling, boxing, tennis, lacrosse
- Football, once play moves inside roughly the 15–20 yard range
- Headshots (zooming in/out lets him keep head size consistent across people with very different head sizes)
- Portraits, family portraits, senior portraits, event/recruiting photography
He says it "smokes" the background even better than a 70–200mm f/2.8 despite the shorter reach at the long end, and ranks it among the two sharpest lenses he's used in his career (the other being the 135mm f/1.8 GM).

**Sony 28–70mm f/2 GM — his other go-to, pairs with the 50–150mm.**
Together the two lenses cover 28–150mm at a constant f/2, which he says replaced what used to be a 4–5 lens prime kit (35mm f/1.4, 50mm f/1.2, 85mm f/1.4, 135mm f/1.8, plus a 70–200mm as backup) for portrait work — same look, far less weight, and no lens-swapping mid-shoot (which he notes also protects the sensor from dust). Used heavily for portraits, medical/office headshots in tight spaces, family portraits, and prom/event photography.

**Long telephoto zooms:**
- **Sony 100–400mm f/4.5–5.6 GM** — light enough to carry all game, sharp even at high ISO under poor high-school gym/stadium lighting.
- **Sony 200–600mm f/5.6–6.3 G** — describes as one of his all-time favorite lenses for wildlife/birds and rugby, where reaching 1200mm effective isn't unusual.
- **Sony 400–800mm f/6.3–8 G** — his newest lens, primarily for bird photography; combined with Sony's clean high-ISO performance, lets him shoot handheld in bright daylight at very high shutter speeds without noise problems.
- He explicitly warns against a specific third-party super-telephoto zoom (a Sigma 300–600mm-class lens): it can't autofocus and zoom at the same time (touching the zoom ring halts AF), and it caps frame rate well below what high-speed Sony bodies can do — a dealbreaker for action work despite being optically sharp.

**Primes he still owns but rarely reaches for:**
- **400mm f/2.8 GM** — his historic workhorse; many of his career-defining images (including a Pulitzer-finalist shot and a Sports Illustrated cover) were shot on 400mm f/2.8 primes going back to the manual-focus film era.
- **135mm f/1.8 GM** — "in my top 5 lenses ever" for sharpness; used to be his favorite lens until the 50–150mm arrived.
- **85mm f/1.4 GM** — kept mainly as a lighter alternative when weight matters (e.g., a two-hour football game shooting handheld).
- Also referenced fondly: old Nikon manual-focus glass and Leica M-mount lenses from his film days, which he says had noticeably better highlight recovery/tonality in blown skies than anything else available at the time.

**Cameras:** Sony A9 III, A1 (and A1 Mark II), and A9 II are his current top picks for sports — praised mainly for extremely clean high-ISO output (he showed usable images at 12,800–16,000 ISO) and fast, reliable autofocus/eye-tracking that works through helmets, face masks, and reflections.

## 2. Best Practices / Technique

- **Back-button autofocus, always.** He separates focus from the shutter button entirely. Holding the back-focus button gives continuous (AF-C) tracking; releasing it locks focus like AF-S — so there's no need to ever switch AF modes. He's emphatic that shutter-button-triggered autofocus ("focus and then the shutter fires") is a mistake for action work, since a missed focus lock means a missed shutter release at the exact wrong moment.
- **Tracking box / "stickiness" setting matched to how cluttered the scene is.** Sony's tracking autofocus has a tenacity/stickiness scale (roughly 1–5). He runs a medium setting (around 4) for most sports as a balance between locking onto a subject and being able to re-acquire quickly. Cleaner, one-on-one situations (e.g., singles tennis) can run stickier/slower to switch; busy scenes with lots of bodies crossing the frame (basketball, with referees and other players) need a faster-to-release setting so focus doesn't jump to the wrong person.
- **Pick your subject before the play develops, then hold it.** He locks onto the ball carrier (or a specific defensive player) as soon as they're live, holds the back-button through the whole play, and lets go only once it's over. He deliberately frames with room in the direction of travel (not centered) so a tackle/impact is already inside the frame.
- **Long lenses need shutter speed, not just good glass.** His rule of thumb for anything past roughly 300–400mm: raise ISO as needed to get shutter speed up (he cites 1/2500–1/6400 sec at 600–800mm) — otherwise camera-shake-induced blur gets misread as "the lens isn't sharp" or "autofocus failed." His diagnostic trick: if literally nothing in the frame is sharp, it's motion/shake; if something else in the frame is sharp but your subject isn't, that's an actual focus miss.
- **No auto-ISO, ever.** He shoots fully manual and rides shutter speed and ISO by hand as light changes (e.g., outdoor field sports under moving clouds), arguing that auto-ISO hands exposure control back to the camera.
- **Auto white balance, left alone.** He uses Sony's "AWB — white priority" option almost exclusively and says he almost never touches color correction afterward.
- **Headshot sessions at volume:** shoot at wide apertures (flash barely has to work, often around 1/64–1/128 power, so batteries last for hundreds/thousands of frames); take 6–8 frames per person minimum, up to ~20 for people prone to blinking; use a zoom rather than a fixed lens so every subject's head size in frame stays consistent regardless of how close they stand or how big their head is.
- **Shallow-DOF portraits at f/1.4–f/2:** watch for only one eye landing in focus when the subject's face isn't perfectly square to the camera. His fix is either to stop down or lean on the constant-f/2 zoom (already a reasonably fast, safer aperture) instead of a faster prime. For very close, very shallow shots, he'll sometimes fire a burst while the subject makes small, slow head movements and pick the sharpest frame afterward, rather than trying to nail focus on a single exposure.
- **Lighting kept deliberately simple:** almost always a single off-camera strobe (Westcott ~400Ws) into a large modifier (36" beauty dish or a light bank), often with a grid to control spill, combined with ambient sky/daylight as fill — not multi-light setups. He credits his ability to work fast (e.g., a full portrait series shot in about 40 minutes) to years of only getting 10–15 minutes per subject in his editorial career.

## 3. How He Works as a Photographer

- **Genre range:** football, basketball, volleyball, wrestling, boxing, tennis, lacrosse, and track & field for a university client; senior portraits, headshots, family portraits, prom photos, medical/corporate office portraits, and large-scale recruiting/networking events; wildlife and bird photography as a personal pursuit.
- **Kit philosophy:** favors owning fewer, more versatile constant-aperture zooms (the 28–70mm f/2 and 50–150mm f/2) over a bag of primes. Says this cuts the weight and cost of carrying 4–5 fast primes, eliminates the risk of sensor dust from frequent lens changes, and still delivers effectively the same shallow-depth-of-field look for 95% of his work. He's largely sold off his other primes but has kept a 135mm f/1.8 and an 85mm f/1.4 for the rare situations (or weight-sensitive days) where they still make sense.
- **Speed as a professional skill:** repeatedly frames working fast as the actual differentiator — full lit portrait sessions in well under an hour, headshot marathons of 100+ people in a day, capturing "five-minute interview" recruiting events for hundreds of participants without complicated setups. He attributes this to his editorial background, where a subject's time was never guaranteed beyond about 15 minutes.
- **Reading the venue instead of fighting it:** for cluttered environments (a packed ballroom of recruiting-event attendees, a small pathology office with no room to back up), he leans on the compression of his long zooms — and, where needed, physically awkward camera positions (bracing the camera against furniture, shooting one-handed against a bookshelf) — rather than adding more gear or lighting to solve the background problem.
- **Long career context:** work spans from manual-focus film cameras and Leica rangefinders through the DSLR era into current mirrorless bodies, including a Sports Illustrated cover credit and a Pulitzer Prize runner-up image — used throughout the talk to frame how much lens/sensor technology has changed what's practical to shoot handheld today versus decades ago.
