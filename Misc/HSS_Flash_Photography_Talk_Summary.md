# Outside Flash Photography Talk — Summary Notes
### Topic: High-Speed Sync (HSS) Flash for Outdoor Portraits

These notes summarize a hands-on demonstration by a professional photographer (Sony shooter, works in sports and higher-education/corporate portraiture) covering how high-speed sync flash works, the gear he uses, the camera/flash settings he dialed in live, and questions from the audience.

---

## 1. What HSS Actually Does

High-Speed Sync (HSS) lets a flash be used at shutter speeds faster than a camera's normal flash sync speed (typically around 1/200–1/250 sec).

- **Without HSS:** At the sync speed or below, the shutter is fully open when the flash fires, so the whole sensor gets lit in one pop. Above the sync speed, the shutter becomes a moving slit (the two curtains overlap), so a single flash pop only lights a thin strip of the frame — the rest is dark.
- **With HSS:** The flash doesn't fire once — it pulses rapidly (many times per second) for the whole duration the shutter slit is traveling across the sensor. Because the pulses are so fast, the eye can't perceive them as separate flashes, and the result is even exposure across the entire frame even at very high shutter speeds (he demonstrated up to 1/8000 sec).
- **The trade-off:** The faster the shutter speed, the more pulses the flash has to fire to cover the full travel time — which means each pulse is weaker and the flash's *effective* usable power output drops significantly. In other words: higher shutter speed = less real flash power available, even if the unit's rated wattage stays the same. This matters most with smaller, lower-powered lights in bright sun.

**Audience question — "Could you get the same shot without HSS?"**
Technically yes, using a strong neutral-density (ND) filter to cut ambient light and drop back under the sync speed. But you'd need a huge amount of flash power (he estimated ~4,000 watt-seconds) to punch through an ND filter that dark, and you'd likely need to stop down to f/16–f/22 for the ambient exposure — which brings the background into sharp focus (unwanted) instead of blurring it. His conclusion: HSS + a wide aperture is a much faster, simpler way to get a shallow-depth-of-field portrait outdoors.

**HSS compatibility notes:**
- Both the camera body and the flash unit need to support HSS — it's not automatic. On his Sony body, a menu setting has to be enabled to unlock shutter speeds above the normal sync limit.
- Third-party flash/trigger systems (he mentioned Godox and Westcott) work fine for HSS as long as the specific flash model is HSS-capable — the trigger and light both need to support it, and it isn't brand-exclusive.
- **Firmware matters:** there are three separate firmware versions that all need to be current — camera body, wireless trigger, and the flash/light unit itself. A camera that hasn't been updated in a couple of years is often the reason gear "isn't working right." Update firmware first when troubleshooting.

## 2. Gear Used in the Demo

- **Camera:** Sony mirrorless body (he referenced the Sony A1 Mark II elsewhere as having the best viewfinder/EVF experience he's used — important for fast panning in sports work).
- **Lens:** Sony 50–150mm f/2 G Master — his favorite portrait lens; a fast constant-aperture zoom he considers the most versatile lens made. He contrasted it with carrying four separate primes (50mm f/1.2, 85mm f/1.4, 135mm f/1.8, 70–200mm f/2.8) — the zoom replaces all of them at a comparable or lower total cost and lighter overall weight.
- **Flash:** Westcott FJ100 — a compact, battery-powered 100 watt-second monolight (about the size of a soda can). Brand new to him at the time of the talk. Has a standard 1/4"-20 thread on the base for mounting.
- **Modifier mount:** An umbrella-swivel bracket (he prefers Westcott's version over Godox's) to attach the light to a stand and angle it freely.
- **Light modifier:** A 36" beauty dish — his all-time favorite modifier because it's fast to set up and has a white (not silver) interior, which he finds more flattering to skin tones and less prone to harsh reflections. He fitted it with a front diffusion sock and paid attention to how far back the flash tube sat inside the dish (positioned it to maximize light bouncing/mixing before it exits).
- **Trigger:** Wireless radio trigger (Westcott's system in this case). Sony bodies use a proprietary hot-shoe with extra pins, so Sony-specific triggers are needed; other brands use a more universal hot-shoe.
- **Other lights in the Westcott FJ line he discussed:**
  - **FJ200** — about a half-stop-plus more powerful than the FJ100.
  - **FJ400 Mark II** — his recommended "universal" light for serious outdoor work; enough power for most situations without the bulk of the 800.
  - **FJ800** — big and heavy; he feels most photographers don't actually need it. For the rare situation needing more output outdoors, he'll instead run two lights (e.g., an FJ400 + FJ100) bounced into the same modifier rather than buy the 800.
  - The FJ light also comes in a speedlight (on-camera-style, hot-shoe-mount) configuration using the same flash tube — he prefers the monolight/strobe version because he virtually never puts flash on-camera.
- **Channels/Groups setup:** He runs on channel 25 of roughly 32 available channels (each photographer picks an unused channel to avoid cross-triggering other photographers' flashes at a shared event). Within a setup he keeps a consistent naming convention: **Group A** = primary/key light on the subject, **Group B** = secondary/fill light, **Group C** = background light.

## 3. Camera & Flash Settings Walked Through Live

**Setup 1 — subject against open sky/background:**
- Test 1: 1/3200 sec, f/2.8, ISO 125, flash power 7.0 (on a 1.0–9.0 scale, 9.0 = full power) → underexposed, sky not well balanced.
- Test 2: raised shutter speed two-thirds of a stop to 1/5000 sec, f/2.8, ISO 125, flash power increased to full (9.0) → much better-balanced exposure between subject and sky.
- Takeaway discussed: with a small, lower-powered light source (the 100Ws unit) in bright sun, you have limited room to move the light away from the subject — moving it back loses output you may not be able to recover, so the light generally needs to stay close.

**Setup 2 — different subject, background changed to distant trees/foliage:**
- Camera pushed to its shutter-speed ceiling: 1/8000 sec, f/2.2, ISO 125, flash initially at full power (9.0) to blow out the background and isolate the subject.
- Talked through balancing the same shot at 1/4000 sec vs 1/8000 (roughly a stop-and-a-third difference) to compare a brighter vs. darker background — showed the group both versions side by side.
- Flash power was then walked back down in increments (9.0 → 7.5 → 8.0/8.5) to fine-tune skin exposure once the shutter speed and background brightness were locked in, rather than changing shutter/aperture again.
- With the fast f/2 lens at close range, he was careful about focus — noted the shallow depth of field at f/2 on a telephoto can easily leave only one eye sharp, so he repositioned the subject's head/angle to get both eyes in the focus plane.
- General workflow he demonstrated: decide the background exposure first (shutter speed + aperture), then dial the flash power up/down to correctly expose the subject against that fixed background — rather than the reverse.

**Posing/positioning technique mentioned alongside settings:**
- Ask the subject to angle their *feet* toward the light (not just turn their head/shoulders) — turning the head alone causes people to drift back to their original position; feet keep the pose stable.
- With a single light and no fill, aim for "wrap" — position the light so it wraps around the face and produces a catchlight (reflection of the modifier) in *both* eyes, not just one. He described placing the subject roughly one-third into the direct light and two-thirds into the wrap.
- For moving to shade: turn subjects to face dark trees/foliage rather than bright sky so they aren't squinting, then rebalance exposure since the ambient background light drops significantly.

## 4. Other Techniques Discussed

- **Off-camera vs. on-camera flash:** Off-camera flash is described as vastly superior for portrait/wedding work — better shadow control and modeling of the face. On-camera flash is treated as a last resort, reserved for situations where there's physically nowhere else to put the light (his example: motorsports, shooting into a race car's cockpit). On-camera flash at a wedding, in his view, reads as a sign of inexperience.
- **Motion blur with flash outdoors (audience question):** Achievable and used often in sports work (e.g., track and field) to keep the subject sharp while limbs blur. Requires test shots first, since this technique is very easy to overexpose. The right shutter speed depends on subject speed — e.g., in oval-track motorsports, roughly 1/125–1/250 sec works in the corners, but a car on the straightaway needs something more like 1/1000–1/2000 sec for a comparable wheel-blur effect.
- **ND filters:** He owns variable ND filters but uses them mainly for video, rarely for stills portraiture. His reasoning: NDs physically sit between the lens and subject, get in the way once you're adding off-camera lights, and require adding much more light to compensate. He prefers using HSS with a fast lens to blow out backgrounds instead.
- **Prime lenses vs. zooms (audience question):** Zooms win on versatility, especially in tight spaces (e.g., small offices) where you can't back up or fit a large modifier. Fast constant-aperture zooms (he cited the Sony/Canon 28–70mm f/2 and his 50–150mm f/2) blur backgrounds nearly as well as primes while eliminating the need to carry/own several separate prime lenses.
- **Glasses while shooting:** He recommends never wearing sunglasses while shooting with lights, since you can't accurately judge exposure through them. For eyeglass wearers using the viewfinder, he pointed out that some Sony bodies let you customize eye relief (push the eyepoint back) as a custom function; he also suggested Zenni Optical for a pair of smaller/closer-fitting prescription glasses dedicated to shooting.

## 5. Gear/Brand Comparisons (Q&A)

- **Westcott vs. Godox:** Prices are similar, but he considers Westcott's customer service much stronger — phone support (Toledo, OH, native English-speaking staff, 8am–6pm Central), advance replacement shipping in emergencies, and in-house repairs. He said Godox gear that fails is often simply discarded rather than repaired. On output, he noted that rated wattage doesn't always match real-world output — he cited the Godox AD600 Pro as producing about a half-stop less light than the Westcott FJ400 despite similar/higher rated wattage.
- **Profoto:** He's openly skeptical of the price premium — cited a Profoto reflector modifier (Magnum reflector) at roughly $750, more than the price of an entire Westcott FJ400 light (~$600). His view is that Profoto's reputation was built through rental-house placement and sponsorship deals with high-profile photographers/educators rather than a output/performance edge.
- **Elinchrome** was mentioned only in passing as another established brand in the same category.

## 6. Business Notes (mentioned briefly)

- Liability insurance is inexpensive relative to its value in bidding larger jobs — he cited roughly $350/year for a $1M policy. On a recent bid for a two-day convention-center shoot (~$6,500), he won the job specifically because he was the only bidder (out of five) who provided a certificate of insurance, even though his bid was the highest.
- His primary client base is higher education (universities/law schools) for portrait and video work; he emphasized being fast and efficient (e.g., producing a finished executive portrait in about 15 minutes) as a selling point.
